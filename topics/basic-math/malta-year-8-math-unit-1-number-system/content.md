# Malta Year 8 Math Unit 1 — Number System

Unit 1 extends Year 7 number work in four steps: powers (1-1), the LCM of three numbers (1-2), square and cube roots (1-3) and recurring decimals (1-4). Each card gives the idea, then a worked example.

**By the end of the unit:** read and work out powers, find the LCM of three numbers, undo squares and cubes, and tell a terminating decimal from a recurring one.

## 1-1 Powers

### 1. Base and exponent

A **power** is repeated multiplication. In $5^3$ the **base** 5 is the number multiplied and the **exponent** 3 says how many fives: $5^3 = 5\times5\times5$.

**Example:** Write $2\times2\times2\times2\times2$ as a power and work it out.

<details>
<summary><b>Working</b></summary>

Five 2s are multiplied, so the base is 2 and the exponent is 5:

$$
2\times2\times2\times2\times2 = 2^5
$$

Double step by step:

$$
2 \to 4 \to 8 \to 16 \to 32, \qquad 2^5 = \boxed{32}
$$

Not $2\times5 = 10$.

</details>

### 2. Powers of 10

Each power of 10 is a 1 followed by that many zeros: $10^2 = 100$, $10^3 = 1000$. Powers of 10 match the place-value columns.

**Example:** Write 4,000,000 as a digit times a power of 10, and work out $3\times10^4$.

<details>
<summary><b>Working</b></summary>

4,000,000 is 4 followed by six zeros:

$$
4{,}000{,}000 = \boxed{4\times10^6}
$$

$10^4$ is 1 followed by four zeros:

$$
3\times10^4 = 3\times10{,}000 = \boxed{30{,}000}
$$

</details>

### 3. Powers inside a calculation

In BIDMAS the **I** (indices) comes before × and ÷, so a power is worked out before multiplying — unless a bracket says otherwise.

**Example:** Calculate $2\times3^2$ and $(2\times3)^2$.

<details>
<summary><b>Working</b></summary>

Power first, then multiply:

$$
2\times3^2 = 2\times9 = \boxed{18}
$$

Bracket first, then the power — a different answer:

$$
(2\times3)^2 = 6^2 = \boxed{36}
$$

</details>

## 1-2 LCM of Three Numbers

### 4. Listing multiples

The **lowest common multiple (LCM)** of several numbers is the smallest positive number that each of them divides exactly.

**Example:** Find the LCM of 4, 6 and 9.

<details>
<summary><b>Working</b></summary>

List multiples until one number is in all three lists:

$$
\begin{aligned} 4&:\ 4,\ 8,\ 12,\ \dots,\ 32,\ 36 \\ 6&:\ 6,\ 12,\ 18,\ 24,\ 30,\ 36 \\ 9&:\ 9,\ 18,\ 27,\ 36 \end{aligned}
$$

36 is the first number in every list:

$$
\text{LCM}(4, 6, 9) = \boxed{36}
$$

</details>

### 5. Using prime factors

Write each number as a product of primes. The LCM uses **every prime that appears, at its highest power**.

**Example:** Find the LCM of 12, 15 and 20.

<details>
<summary><b>Working</b></summary>

Prime factors:

$$
\begin{aligned} 12 &= 2^2\times3 \\ 15 &= 3\times5 \\ 20 &= 2^2\times5 \end{aligned}
$$

Take $2^2$, 3 and 5, each at its highest power:

$$
\text{LCM} = 2^2\times3\times5 = \boxed{60}
$$

</details>

### 6. When repeating events line up

Events that repeat every $a$, $b$ and $c$ minutes happen together again after $\text{LCM}(a, b, c)$ minutes.

**Example:** Three bus routes leave Valletta together at 8:00. They run every 10, 12 and 15 minutes. When do all three next leave together?

<details>
<summary><b>Working</b></summary>

Prime factors:

$$
10 = 2\times5, \qquad 12 = 2^2\times3, \qquad 15 = 3\times5
$$

Highest powers $2^2$, 3, 5:

$$
\text{LCM} = 2^2\times3\times5 = 60 \text{ minutes}
$$

60 minutes after 8:00:

$$
\boxed{9{:}00}
$$

</details>

## 1-3 Square and Cube Roots

### 7. Undoing a square or a cube

A **square root** undoes squaring: $\sqrt{49} = 7$ because $7^2 = 49$. A **cube root** undoes cubing: $\sqrt[3]{27} = 3$ because $3^3 = 27$. The sign $\sqrt{\ }$ means the positive root.

**Example:** Find $\sqrt{196}$ and $\sqrt[3]{216}$.

<details>
<summary><b>Working</b></summary>

Which number squared is 196? $14\times14 = 196$:

$$
\sqrt{196} = \boxed{14}
$$

Which number cubed is 216? $6\times6\times6 = 216$:

$$
\sqrt[3]{216} = \boxed{6}
$$

</details>

### 8. Squares and cubes to know

Knowing the first square and cube numbers makes roots quick:

$$
\begin{aligned} &\text{squares: } 1,\ 4,\ 9,\ 16,\ 25,\ 36, \\ &\qquad 49,\ 64,\ 81,\ 100,\ 121,\ 144 \\ &\text{cubes: } 1,\ 8,\ 27,\ 64,\ 125 \end{aligned}
$$

**Example:** Apart from 1, which number up to 100 is both a square and a cube?

<details>
<summary><b>Working</b></summary>

Look for a number in both lists: 64, because $8^2 = 64$ and $4^3 = 64$.

$$
\boxed{64}
$$

</details>

### 9. Estimating a root

When a number is not a perfect square, its square root lies between the roots of the perfect squares on either side.

**Example:** Between which two whole numbers is $\sqrt{50}$? Which one is it closer to?

<details>
<summary><b>Working</b></summary>

The squares on either side are 49 and 64:

$$
49 < 50 < 64 \quad\Rightarrow\quad 7 < \sqrt{50} < 8
$$

50 is just 1 above 49 but 14 below 64, so the root is just above 7:

$$
\sqrt{50} \approx \boxed{7.1}
$$

</details>

### 10. Roots in area and volume

The side of a square is the square root of its area. The edge of a cube is the cube root of its volume.

**Example:** A square courtyard has an area of $144\ \text{m}^2$, and a cube-shaped box has a volume of $125\ \text{cm}^3$. Find the side of the courtyard and the edge of the box.

<details>
<summary><b>Working</b></summary>

$$
\sqrt{144} = \boxed{12\ \text{m}}
$$

$$
\sqrt[3]{125} = \boxed{5\ \text{cm}}
$$

</details>

## 1-4 Recurring Decimals and Fractions

### 11. Fraction to decimal

A fraction is a division: $\frac38$ means $3\div8$. Some decimals stop (**terminating**); others repeat a digit or a block of digits forever (**recurring**).

**Example:** Write $\frac38$ and $\frac56$ as decimals.

<details>
<summary><b>Working</b></summary>

$3\div8$ stops after three decimal places:

$$
\frac38 = \boxed{0.375}
$$

$5\div6$ leaves remainder 2 again and again, so the 3 repeats forever:

$$
\frac56 = \boxed{0.8333\ldots}
$$

</details>

### 12. Dot notation

A dot over a digit means it repeats. Dots over the first and last digits of a block mean the whole block repeats: $0.\dot{4} = 0.444\ldots$ and $0.\dot{1}\dot{8} = 0.181818\ldots$

**Example:** Write $\frac23$ and $\frac{7}{11}$ in dot notation.

<details>
<summary><b>Working</b></summary>

$2\div3 = 0.666\ldots$:

$$
\frac23 = \boxed{0.\dot{6}}
$$

$7\div11 = 0.636363\ldots$ — the block 63 repeats:

$$
\frac{7}{11} = \boxed{0.\dot{6}\dot{3}}
$$

</details>

### 13. Will the decimal terminate?

Write the fraction in its simplest form. If the denominator has no prime factors other than 2 and 5, the decimal **terminates**; any other prime factor makes it **recur**. ($\frac{3}{12} = \frac14$ terminates, even though 12 has a factor 3.)

**Example:** Which of $\frac{7}{20}$ and $\frac{4}{15}$ terminates?

<details>
<summary><b>Working</b></summary>

$20 = 2^2\times5$ — only 2s and 5s, so it terminates:

$$
\frac{7}{20} = \boxed{0.35}
$$

$15 = 3\times5$ — the 3 makes it recur:

$$
\frac{4}{15} = \boxed{0.2\dot{6}}
$$

</details>

### 14. The ninths pattern

Ninths give one repeating digit: $\frac19 = 0.\dot{1}$, $\frac29 = 0.\dot{2}$, and so on. So a single repeating digit $d$ after the point is $\frac{d}{9}$.

**Example:** Write $0.\dot{7}$ as a fraction, and show that $0.\dot{6} = \frac23$.

<details>
<summary><b>Working</b></summary>

One repeating digit 7:

$$
0.\dot{7} = \boxed{\frac79}
$$

$0.\dot{6} = \frac69$; divide top and bottom by 3:

$$
\frac69 = \boxed{\frac23}
$$

</details>
